Step 6 · Circuits

Decisions

Two groups of neurons race. The first to the finish line makes the choice.

You'll learn

  • How competing neurons turn noisy evidence into one choice
  • Why hard choices take longer and go wrong more often
  • The trade between speed and accuracy
  • The Python behind a classic decision model

The idea

A decision is a tug of war. Each team pulls harder the more evidence it gets, and the first to drag the rope past a line wins.

  • Each group excites itself, so a small lead grows: the same reverberation as Step 5.
  • Each group inhibits the other, so only one can win.
  • A threshold ends the race. Crossing it is the moment of choice.

The task

Dots jiggle on a screen. Some share a direction, the rest move at random. Which way are they going? Coherence is the share moving together: 51% is easy, 3% is very hard, 0% is pure guessing. Monkeys and people have done this task for over 30 years.

How real decisions work

  

    
1. Sensors report the evidence

    
Neurons in visual area MT each prefer one direction. Their firing tracks the dots moment to moment, but it is noisy. One glance is never enough.

  

  

    
2. Deciders add it up

    
    
      
  • Neurons in parietal area LIP ramp up as evidence piles in, faster when the dots are clearer.
    • 
      
  • Monkeys made their choice when LIP firing reached about the same level each time (2002). That's the threshold.
    • 
      
  • Strong self-excitation through slow NMDA synapses lets the circuit add evidence up over hundreds of milliseconds.
    • 
    

  

  

    
3. Speed or accuracy?

    
Tell people to hurry and they answer faster but make more mistakes. In models this is a lower threshold. Brain imaging and Parkinson's research point to the basal ganglia as one place that sets it.

The equations

  

    
Equation 1 · Input to each group

    

      xA = Jself SA − Jother SB + I0 + IA + noise
      IA = Jext μ (1 + c/100)
    

    

      
SHow many of the group's slow NMDA synapses are open, 0 to 1.

      
JselfSelf-excitation: a group that fires keeps itself firing.

      
JotherCompetition: each group inhibits the other.

      
cCoherence, the evidence. Group B gets (1 − c/100).

    

  

  

    
Equation 2 · Input to firing rate

    

      r = (ax − b) / (1 − e−d(ax − b))
    

    
Near silent for small input, then rising in a straight line. It's Step 1's f-I curve, fitted for a whole crowd of spiking neurons.

  

  

    
Equation 3 · Slow synapses add up the evidence

    

      dS/dt = −S/τS + (1 − S) γ r
    

    

      
τS = 100 msNMDA synapses close slowly, so they remember recent firing. That's what lets the circuit add evidence over time.

      
(1 − S)Synapses can't be more than fully open.

    

    
The choice: average each rate over ~20 ms. The first group whose average passes the threshold wins.

    

      All the numbers
      
Jself 0.2609 nA, Jother 0.0497 nA, I0 0.3255 nA, Jext 0.00052 nA/Hz, μ 30 Hz, a 270 Hz/nA, b 108 Hz, d 0.154 s, γ 0.641, τS 100 ms, read-out smoothing 20 ms. Noise: a slowly varying current (time constant 2 ms, size 0.02 nA). Step 0.5 ms. From Wong & Wang (2006), who boiled Wang's 2002 network of 2,000 spiking neurons down to these two numbers.

    Why two numbers instead of 2,000 neurons?
    
Wang's 2002 spiking network made the same choices, but it is slow to run and hard to see inside. Averaging each group and keeping only the slowest variable (NMDA) gives two equations you can plot as a race. It's like Step 5's rate model taken one step further.

  


  
Beyond the lab

  

    
Emerging tech

    
    
      
  • Reading a choice as it forms. In 2021, researchers decoded monkeys' developing decisions in real time from hundreds of neurons recorded at once, and caught changes of mind as they happened.
    • 
      
  • Winner-take-all chips. Competing groups that inhibit each other are a standard building block on neuromorphic chips, used to pick one answer from many.
    • 
      
  • An optimal rule. Adding noisy evidence until a threshold is the fastest way to reach a set accuracy (Wald, 1947). The race above approximates it.
    • 
    

  

  

    
For good

    
    
      
  • Parkinson's. Deep brain stimulation helps patients move, but in a 2007 study it made them rush hard choices. Its threshold-setting role in the basal ganglia explains why.
    • 
      
  • Mental health research. Fitting race models to people's choices is used to study ADHD, depression and anxiety. It's a research tool, not yet a diagnosis.
    • 
      
  • Mental privacy. If choices can be read before they're made, who may read them? Chile (2021) and Colorado (2024) now protect brain data by law.
    • 
    

  


  
Key terms

  

    

      
Coherence
Share of dots moving the same way: the strength of the evidence.

      
Evidence accumulation
Adding up noisy information over time before choosing.

      
Threshold
The level of firing that triggers the choice.

      
Winner-take-all
Competing groups inhibit each other until only one stays active.

      
Speed–accuracy trade-off
Deciding faster means more mistakes, and vice versa.

      
LIP
Lateral intraparietal area: neurons that ramp up as a choice forms.

      
Psychometric curve
Accuracy plotted against how strong the evidence is.
