The idea

A neuron is a leaky bucket. Input fills it, a hole drains it. When it reaches the brim, it tips over: a spike.

  • All or nothing. Every spike is the same size.
  • Weak input fades. It leaks out before reaching the brim.
  • Stronger input means more spikes, not bigger ones.

How sodium and potassium make a spike


  

    
1. Why ions want to move

    
Pumps keep the two sides of the membrane uneven:

    

      
Sodium (Na⁺)~10× more outsideWants to rush inPulls voltage toward +50 mV

      
Potassium (K⁺)~30× more insideWants to leak outPulls voltage toward −77 mV

    

    
Whichever ion has more open channels wins the tug-of-war. At rest, mostly potassium channels are open, so the cell sits near −65 mV, close to potassium's −77.

  


  

    
2. The channels have gates

    

      
m
Sodium “opener”: fast. Opens when voltage rises.

      
h
Sodium “plug”: slow. Closes when voltage rises.

      
n
Potassium door: slow. Opens when voltage rises.

    

    
Each gate is a number from 0 (shut) to 1 (open). Sodium flows only when the opener is open and the plug is out.

  


  

    
3. A spike in four stages

    
    
      
  1. Rest · −65 mVSodium openers shut (m = 0.05). Some potassium open (n = 0.32).
    2. 
      
  2. Rise · up to +41 mV in ~1 msInput nudges V up → openers fly open (m = 0.89) → sodium rushes in → V rises more. A chain reaction.
    3. 
      
  3. Fall · back down in ~2 msThe slow plug closes (h = 0.09) and stops sodium. Potassium doors open (n = 0.77) and K⁺ leaves.
    4. 
      
  4. Undershoot · −76 mV, ~10 ms to recoverPotassium doors are slow to shut, so V dips below rest. The plug is still in (h = 0.11), so it can't fire: the refractory period.
    5. 
    

    
Watch all four stages live in the gate trace in Demo 2.

The Hodgkin–Huxley equations

  
Two equations describe everything above. Hodgkin and Huxley fitted them to a squid's giant nerve in 1952, and won the 1963 Nobel Prize. Demo 2 runs them exactly.


  

    
Equation 1 · The voltage

    
How fast the voltage changes = input − three currents.

    

      C · dV/dt = I
      − gNa m³h (V − ENa)
      − gK n⁴ (V − EK)
      − gL (V − EL)
    

    

      
C dV/dtRate of voltage change. C is the membrane's capacitance: how much charge it stores.

      
IInput current: the slider and the zaps.

      
gNa = 120Sodium conductance if every door were open. Gates scale it down.

      
m³hFraction of sodium channels open. Three openers and the plug must allow it: m×m×m×h.

      
n⁴Fraction of potassium channels open. Four parts must all open.

      
(V − E)Driving force. How far V is from the ion's target voltage (+50 for Na, −77 for K). Further away = stronger push.

      
gL(V − EL)A small leak that's always open.

    

  


  

    
Equation 2 · The gates

    
Each gate opens and closes at speeds set by the voltage.

    

      dm/dt = αm(V) (1 − m) − βm(V) m
    

    

      
αm(V)Opening speed. Grows as V rises.

      
(1 − m)The gates still shut, which are able to open.

      
βm(V)Closing speed.

      
mThe gates already open, which are able to close.

    

    
The same equation runs for h and n, each with its own speeds. m is ~10× faster than h and n. That timing difference is the whole spike: sodium opens first, then the plug and potassium catch up.

  


  

    All the numbers
    

      
Rates per ms, V in mV (resting at −65).

      

        αm = 0.1(V+40) / (1 − e−(V+40)/10)
        βm = 4 e−(V+65)/18
        αh = 0.07 e−(V+65)/20
        βh = 1 / (1 + e−(V+35)/10)
        αn = 0.01(V+55) / (1 − e−(V+55)/10)
        βn = 0.125 e−(V+65)/80
      

      

        
C1 µF/cm²

        
gNa, gK, gL120, 36, 0.3 mS/cm²

        
ENa, EK, EL+50, −77, −54.4 mV

    Why use the simpler bucket?
    

      
The bucket costs about 3 sums per time step. Real channels cost about 30. For circuits of thousands of neurons (Step 3 onward), the bucket keeps a laptop fast enough.

      
Left out on purpose: dendrite branches, dozens of other channel types, neurotransmitters, energy use.

    

  


  
Key terms

  

    

      
Membrane potential
Voltage inside the cell compared with outside.

      
Resting potential
About −70 mV when the neuron is quiet.

      
Threshold
The voltage where a spike becomes unstoppable.

      
Action potential (spike)
A 1 ms all-or-nothing voltage pulse.

      
Ion channel
A protein door that lets one kind of ion through.

      
Gate (m, h, n)
The moving part of a channel. 0 = shut, 1 = open.

      
Reversal potential (E)
The voltage an ion pushes toward: +50 mV for Na⁺, −77 mV for K⁺.

      
Conductance (g)
How easily current flows through the channels.

      
Refractory period
The short time after a spike when it can't fire.

      
Firing rate
Spikes per second (Hz). Encodes input strength.

    

  


  

    Go deeper: the equation
    

      
τ·dV/dt = −(V − EL) + R·I

      
Change in voltage = leak toward rest + input. The bucket settles at EL + R·I, so it fires when R·I > 15 mV, which is I > 1.5 nA. That's the threshold you found in Demo 1.

      
Computers can't do smooth time, so they take tiny steps (dt = 0.1 ms) and nudge V each step. This is called Euler integration, and it's how most brain simulators work.
